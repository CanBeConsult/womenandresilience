What we believe

Resilience and becoming, at your own pace.

Women who have been abused at work are usually asked to prove it, quietly, alone, to the same institution that allowed it. Most give up. Many leave their careers. Almost none are ever counted.

Women and Resilience starts from the opposite position. We believe women when they speak. We treat what they lived as evidence, not allegation. And we care as much about who women become afterwards as about what was done to them.

Women speak about what they lived, and we listen, we trust. What they say does not become a report, but shared experiences that build a community.

Believed

No woman should have to build a legal case before she is allowed to be upset. Being heard comes first.

Connected

Isolation is how workplace abuse survives. Women who find each other stop believing they are the problem.

Counted

What is not measured is not managed, and what is not managed keeps happening. Every account adds to the record.

As women, we have to accept the truth and say it out loud: workplace abuse is still happening. And the moment we share what we lived, we begin to imagine — and to design — the better future we can build together.

Founder, Women & Resilience

Your safety, and what happens to what you say

Nothing you tell us leaves this community without your permission.

Women are asked to speak and then punished for having spoken. That is the pattern this community exists to break, so the rules about what happens to your words come before anything else we do.

These commitments apply wherever you are and wherever you work. We do not hold one standard in one country and a weaker one somewhere else.

We do not pass it on

What you share is not sold, traded, published or handed to an employer, a regulator or the press. Not in exchange for funding, and not in exchange for coverage.

Consent is specific

Agreeing to talk to us is not agreeing to be quoted. Agreeing to be quoted is not agreeing to be named. Each step is asked for separately, and you can withdraw.

Aggregated, not identified

Anything used publicly or in advocacy is reported as a pattern across many women. Identifying details are removed before anything leaves us.

We hold the minimum

We ask for what the work needs and nothing more. You are never required to name your employer or your colleagues in order to be heard.

Safety before evidence

If holding a piece of information would put a woman at risk, it does not get held. No data point is worth more than the woman who gave it.

A community with rules

Women are believed here, and spoken to with care. Naming and shaming, pile-ons, and advice dressed up as legal or medical counsel have no place in it.

The podcast

Women narrating their own record.

Long-form conversations, hosted by a survivor, with women who have lived workplace abuse and come out the other side of it. Episodes are organised not by industry but by the shape the abuse took, because that is how women recognise themselves in someone else's story.

Nothing is broadcast without consent, and consent can be withdrawn. Nothing that would identify an employer or a colleague goes to air without legal review. The subject is what it did to their lives, and what they built afterwards.

In development. A French-language edition is planned.

If you are thinking of speaking

You do not need to have decided. This is what happens, in order, and you can stop at any step.

  1. A private first conversation

    It is not recorded, and it commits you to nothing. Agreeing to talk to us is not agreeing to be on the podcast.

  2. You choose how you are known

    Your first name, another name, or no name. Your voice can be altered if you ask.

  3. You hear it before anyone else

    Nothing airs until you have listened to the edited episode and agreed to it. You keep control of what airs.

  4. You can change your mind

    Nothing is broadcast without consent, and consent can be withdrawn.

You are never asked to name an employer or a colleague. A few lines are enough to begin, and you are welcome to ask a question first.

The record we are building together

Nothing changes until it is counted.

The global evidence that exists counts how often workplace abuse happens, and in what form. It does not follow what happens to women afterwards. Every woman who shares her experience adds one line to that missing half of the record: what abuse costs in health, in careers, in families and in money.

You share at your own pace and in your own words, and you stop whenever you want. You are never asked to name your employer. You decide what is kept and what is not.

What comes back is aggregated — patterns, not people — and used in three places: the podcast, public awareness work, and advocacy with the organisations, regulators and legislators who set what employers are required to do. Since 2019 there has been an international standard to point at: ILO Convention No. 190, the first global instrument on violence and harassment in the world of work.

What we fight for

Prevention and healing.

Across many countries, workplace harassment policy has been rewritten, trained on and signed off as a compliance element. Women still leave jobs, careers and health behind from trauma. A policy is compliance, it is not prevention nor risk management.

What is missing sits underneath the policy: impact management and measurement, prevention guidance, culturally informed tools, caring behaviours, mutual understanding, mental health support that arrives early, public awareness, and above all usable baseline data to support change, understand impact and required changes.

Prevention starts in listening, understanding, education, leadership, and in the slow work of restoring trust in a workplace after it has been broken. We intend to make workplace abuse a term people use plainly, wherever they work, and to put the evidence in the hands of the people who write the rules.

22.8%

of people in employment — around 743 million — have experienced at least one form of violence and harassment at work during their working life: physical, psychological or sexual.

International Labour Organization, Lloyd's Register Foundation and Gallup. Experiences of Violence and Harassment at Work: A global first survey. Geneva, 5 December 2022. Drawn from the Lloyd's Register Foundation World Risk Poll 2021, 121 countries and territories.

8.2%

of women reported sexual violence and harassment at work, against 5.0% of men — by far the widest gap between women and men of any form measured.

ILO, Lloyd's Register Foundation and Gallup, 2022, as above. Young women were twice as likely as young men to report it, and migrant women almost twice as likely as non-migrant women.

3 in 5

of those who had experienced violence and harassment at work had experienced it more than once. It is rarely an incident. It is usually a pattern.

ILO, Lloyd's Register Foundation and Gallup, 2022, as above. For most respondents the most recent incident had taken place within the previous five years.

What we are working towards

Being heard

Women listened to at the point they need it, without having to prove themselves first.

Community

Women connected to other women. Networks of lived experience that carry resilience rather than isolation.

A shared record

Baseline data on the impact of workplace abuse on women's lives, gathered with consent and held safely.

Awareness

Podcast conversations that carry lived experience into public view and make workplace abuse a term people use plainly.

Advocacy

Evidence placed with the organisations and legislators already working for policy change, in any country where women are working.

Culture change

Findings returned to workplaces themselves: prevention, positive leadership and equity, not another compliance module.
